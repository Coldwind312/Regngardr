***Rational Explanation Given:** Earthquake coinciding with bright meteor._

---
_“At 8.38 pm on 23 January 1974, an earthquake of magnitude 3.5 was felt over a wide area of north Wales and as far as Liverpool. Since earthquakes are uncommon in the UK, it was not generally recognized for what it was, and since a number of unusual lights in the sky had been observed the same evening, it was considered possible that an aircraft had crashed, or a meteorite had impacted. Further confusion was caused by lights seen on the Berwyn Mountains, which subsequently turned out to have belonged to poachers.”_

— Wikipedia; [Berwyn Mountain Range - Alleged UFO Crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berwyn_range#Alleged_UFO_crash%5D)

---
It may be that you’ve heard of the famed ‘Roswell Incident’, when, in 1947, New Mexico rancher Mac Brazel found the wreckage of… _something_… that had crashed on his land near the town of Roswell. He reported the find to his local sheriff who notified the nearby Army Air Force base (the AAF being the World War II precursor to the USAF). The AAF put out an announcement that they had found a ‘flying disc’—as stock UFOs were referred to at the time—and almost immediately followed that up with a further announcement that actually no, it wasn’t a flying disc, but rather a simple, ordinary ‘weather balloon’.

For a while the story died down, but then in the 1970s, with the Cold War at a precarious height, people got interested in UFOs again, and the Roswell story was dug up and made to serve a massive conspiracy narrative building up at the time. I say ‘narrative’, but that might suggest a single coherent storyline, which this wasn’t. This was a bundle of vaguely related accusations against the US government, fed by distrust stemming from, among other things, the Watergate scandal and the catastrophic Vietnam war. It built on the idea that the UFO phenomenon was alien in nature—that UFOs were spaceships carrying beings from another world. This had been a popular idea for some time, obviously: ever since the earliest sci-fi writers had looked to the skies and wondered what would happen if something terribly powerful came down and fucked us all the way up. (And when I say ‘sci-fi writers’, I mean the likes of HG Wells, who had Things To Say about the hubris of empire—but even then, the sci-fi trappings were only evolving from earlier notions of gods and monsters doing unto us likewise.)

Back to Roswell. Why yes, I *do* have ADHD, thank you. Anyway it was during the 1970s and 80s that the malevolent government conspiracy idea really got a grip on ‘ufology’, and led into its approximate pop-culture peak in the 1990s, when we got _The X Files_, which kind of became the definitive version of this legend. But we’re in the 1970s: Roswell suddenly lit up, and lots of wild claims and speculation developed around it. UFO cover-ups (covers-up?) were the big ticket for writers and commentators—the bloggers and podcasters of their day—and it was in the 1970s, 1974 to be exact, when Britain, specifically Wales, got its own Roswell Incident.

“Nice dissolve.”

Wales is a hilly and mountainous country that borders England to the west. It’s very approximately rectangular, albeit with a big bay cutting a chunk out of its western side, with three of its edges coastline and largely free of having England hanging off them. It’s got some serious topography: by British standards it’s rugged and mountainous (the Alps and the Himalayas are pointing and laughing, but we’re fond of the bumpy terrain we have, okay?). Back in the days when it used to rain, it’d rain quite a lot in Wales. It was fairly famous for it.

The Berwyn mountain range is named for the peak of Cadair Berwyn, a summit 832 metres above sea level. And it’s this mountain, just beside the village of Llandrillo in Denbighshire, into which it’s alleged that a UFO crashed, Roswell-style, on a cold night in January 1974.

Reportedly, local residents heard a loud thundery noise and felt a shaking of the ground at 2038 hours on the evening of January 23. Those outdoors reported a bright light, a glow among the mountains, and one, at a farm about 35 km from Berwyn peak, claimed to have seen a ‘sphere’ coming down into the hills. Many witnesses to sight, sound or sensation immediately rang the local authorities to report an explosion, with many suggesting a possible aircraft crash. Emergency services and RAF search and rescue teams were dispatched and scoured the hills but came up with nothing—if, that is, you believe the records, and not the immediate and persistent rumours about how the military closed off a section of the mountain. Or of how Certain Things were taken off the mountain and squirrelled away to military facilities at RAF Rudloe Manor in Wiltshire, or to Porton Down near Salisbury.

These sites are both occasionally presented as ‘the UK’s Area-51’, given their use for secretive defence projects. Porton Down is particularly notorious as the site of the Defence Science and Technology Laboratory, an agency of the UK’s Ministry of Defence, and has featured in pop culture as the focus of many an ‘experiments getting out of control’ story.

At Berwyn, then, we have the basis of a story: something fell from the sky, landing on the mountain with a resounding explosion and a flare of light. Shortly thereafter, the military turned up, sealed off the area, and took something away.

Does indeed sound pretty Roswell, dunnit? A remarkably similar tale.

I’d consider there to be two valid, if not sound, arguments here. If—and that’s a [full-on Greek ‘if’](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laconic_phrase) there: εἰ, no less—spaceships had crashed at Roswell and Berwyn, and εἰ the governments of each state, the US and the UK, shared a cover-up approach to such events even across thirty years, then one would expect the narratives to be approximately similar.

If, on the other hand, public reporting of the Berwyn Mountain incident occurred with broadening pop-culture awareness of the Roswell incident, and against a background of emerging conspiracy theories about government covers-up (cover-ups?) of aliens, then one would still expect the narratives to be approximately similar.

The reality is that there are no solidly reliable accounts of anything that happened that night. There are individual reports from witnesses such as Huw Lloyd, who was 14 at the time and lived in the area. He reported hearing a ‘thump’ which was followed by “the whole place […] shaking quite violently, like an earth tremor”[^1].

Huw told the History Channel’s _Ancient Aliens_ show that he’d been taken out to see the site and had observed a ‘pulsating’ and ‘otherworldly’ light, but wasn’t allowed to go near it. While I would not wish to cast nasturtiums on Mr Lloyd’s character and integrity, I feel no such compunctions about pointing out that the History Channel is an extremely un-serious producer of absolute unmitigated omnicredulous sensationalist bollocks and will happily propagate absolutely any bonkers notion about aliens if it thinks it’ll get some views out of it. If it involves Hitler or the ancient Egyptians, or the Annunaki, so much the better. The _Ancient Aliens_ show is all of that, what I’ve just said about the History Channel in general, but amplified to the point that it does to your brain what the [speaker in Doc Brown's garage](https://youtu.be/YvfHNhkSOG0) did to Marty McFly.[^2]

Pat Evans, a local nurse, guessed that an aircraft had crashed. She said that she went out with her daughters to see if anyone needed medical help and they observed a ‘glowing dome of light in the distance’, and that it was ‘pulsing’[^3]. But it faded away before they reached the site.

Reportedly (and as would be expected), emergency services, mountain rescue and military SAR units were called to the area and searched for a crash site. They found nothing.

A few days later the Institute of Geological Science confirmed that an earthquake had struck the area, with a 3.5 reading on the then-in-use local magnitude ‘Richter’ scale.

Roger Musson of the British Geological Survey notes in a 2006 report on the incident[^4] that various explanations had been considered at the time, including the detonation of an unexploded wartime bomb, though this last was discounted in favour of the more likely meteor scenarios. A large greenish light had been seen crossing the sky towards North Wales from observations in Anglesey, the Isle of Man and Cumbria. Okay. So that something was seen in the sky is a pretty solid conclusion. And that something shook the earth is equally so. Presumably the one caused the other?

Musson mentions that “many of the light phenomena”—the reports of ‘strange lights’ that helped make the incident into a story—“were reported after the tremor”. He cites the above-mentioned report which described a ‘flying sphere’ but notes that this was received almost an hour and a half after the event; and one account which was carried in the local press and talked of a ‘disc with revolving coloured lights’ had been reported at 0915 the morning after the incident from someone 25 kilometres from the site.

By 2330 the night of the incident the police had withdrawn their search teams and notified the RAF that nothing had been found, though they did report seeing some lights south of the area, which were subsequently attributed to poachers operating in the area.

Follow-up searches were made in daylight, and the last called off at 1415 on 24 January.

The mythology of the incident has evolved to include the detail that an area of the mountainside was sealed off such that civilian agencies, including the police, were denied access. This is usually cited as evidence that the military were recovering something. But it is mythology only: the police were all over the area for several hours after the incident, as multiple agencies’ logs record. Conspiracists will obviously say, “Well, yes, but logs can be falsified”—but the problem with that kind of thinking is that if you take it to its conclusion it leaves you without reason to accept that anything happened at all. Anywhere. Ever. Maybe Wales doesn’t even exist. After all, you only have other people’s word to go on, and they could all be in on it. And okay, you can claim you’ve grown up in Wales, that you’ve lived there all your life and that you’re intimately familiar with every square metre of the country. But you only know what you’ve been _told_ is Wales. They might have put you in some other place when you were born and everyone you’ve met since is just a paid actor there to convince you you live in Wales.

See how dumb that thinking gets?

Let’s try to put dumb aside here.

In the end, you have to be willing to take things at face value unless you’ve got reason not to, because otherwise it’s pointless investigating anything. So. It’s reasonable to conclude that the numerous reports of the loud noise and vibration experienced in the area were reporting accurately. They cross-check, they share consistencies, and they can be validated by external mechanisms: namely the seismometers that picked up the waves.

Something *did* go bangedy-rumble, and it made the ground go shakey-shakey for a minute.

It’s not unreasonable to suppose that there was an anomalous light in the sky. Again, it was reported from several locations and tracked along a course that would have put it around the right location for an impact. But nothing was found. No crater. No meteorite. Weird, right?

Well, not really. Meteors can make a hell of a bang and big shiny light even when they don’t reach the ground. In 2013, a 9,000-tonne lump of rock and iron fell into the atmosphere over Russia’s Chelyabinsk oblast. It was doing just fine until it got to about 23 kilometres in altitude, when the pressure proved too much and it went extremely bang, to the tune of about 470,000 tonnes of TNT. Despite being so high up, the Chelyabinsk meteor still caused ground damage over a hundred kilometres and indirectly injured nearly 1,500 people.

And it never—at least as one object—hit the ground. Fragments did, and were subsequently collected; and the shock from the explosion definitely left a mark. But it didn’t leave a single classic impact crater at a single site. A smaller bolide, less explosive force, or higher altitude could easily have left Chelyabinsk residents wondering what that tremendous bang and the momentary bright flare of light might have been, without any evidence left on the ground at all.

Back to Berwyn.

So, we’ve basically wrapped it up, right? A meteor fell over the area and exploded in the air, a la Chelyabinsk or 1908’s still-more-infamous Tunguska incident, and that accounts for everything. No alien spaceships required. All sorted. All rational.

Eh.

Kind of.

There’s just one problem. The seismological monitoring coverage of the UK might’ve been a bit sparse back then; but it was good enough to be able to say that the shaking of the ground was due to an actual earthquake. A 3.5-magnitude quake that cracked somewhere between 7.5-15 km below Llyn Tegid at Bala, around 10 km west-ish of Landrillo, where I’ve been doing my simulated pixture-takin’. It’d be very easy, as suggested above, to assume that the Bang was a result, impact or airburst, of the meteor.

But it wasn’t.

Seismologists are pretty confident that the Bala earthquake occurred _as well as_ the meteor. And, moreover, that they occurred _at the same time_.

There were no aliens at Berwyn Mountain in 1974. There was no effort by the UK military or NATO or anyone else to recover a Crashed Alien Spaceship. I’m pretty confident we can rule all that sort of thing out.

But a prominent meteor fell and an earthquake struck in the same part of Earth at exactly the same time. And that seems weird. Not _impossible_—that’s important: there are tonnes of rocks zipping around up there, and a lot of them drop into our atmosphere every day, mostly burning up unnoticed. And the ground beneath our feet might feel solid enough, but anyone who’s experienced an earthquake, or even a hefty tremor, knows how disconcerting it can be when that nice solid reliable ground suddenly isn’t those things. Still, seismic activity is pretty much constant around the world, albeit most of it we don’t really notice. But for all that’s, it’s pretty common.

Each one of these events on its own is pretty common. And tiny earthquakes happening at the same time and place as tiny meteors? Also common. Probably an everyday thing, to be honest. But in this case it’s the simultaneity and the bigness together that make us look twice. That make us think, “That’s weird.”

*Is* it weird, though? Was there some sort of connection beyond the mundane? Or was it just a freakish coincidence? That I can’t tell you. But if there’s mystery attached to this particular incident, that’s probably where it sits.
